Few plants reward a little attention the way tomatoes do. A single well-tended plant can produce more fruit than most households can eat fresh, and the flavor difference between a homegrown tomato and a grocery-store one is hard to overstate. Here's what actually matters if you want a good harvest, whether you're working with a full garden bed or a couple of pots on a balcony.

## Choosing the right variety

Not every tomato is suited to every space. Determinate varieties, like Roma or Celebrity, grow to a set size and produce most of their fruit within a few weeks — good for containers and small gardens. Indeterminate varieties, like Cherokee Purple or Sungold, keep growing and producing until frost, but they need more room and sturdier support.

If this is your first season, start with a reliable, disease-resistant variety rather than the most exciting heirloom you can find. Confidence built from an easy first harvest will carry you into more ambitious varieties next year.

## Planting: timing and depth

Tomatoes are warm-season plants and will sulk — or die outright — in cold soil. Wait until night temperatures are reliably above 50°F (10°C) before moving seedlings outdoors.

Plant deeper than feels natural. Tomato stems grow roots wherever they touch soil, so burying two-thirds of the stem (removing the lower leaves first) builds a stronger root system than planting at the same depth as the nursery pot.

## Watering and feeding

Consistency matters more than volume. Irregular watering — a dry week followed by a heavy soak — is the most common cause of blossom end rot and split fruit. Aim for deep, even watering two to three times a week rather than a light sprinkle daily, and water at the soil level rather than overhead to keep foliage dry and reduce disease.

Feed every two to three weeks once flowering starts, using a fertilizer formulated for tomatoes or one lower in nitrogen than phosphorus and potassium. Too much nitrogen produces lush leaves and few tomatoes.

## Supporting and pruning

Even determinate varieties benefit from a cage or stake — it keeps fruit off the ground and improves airflow. For indeterminate varieties, a sturdy stake with regular tying, or a Florida weave for a row of plants, works better than a flimsy cage that will topple under a full-grown plant.

Pinch off the small shoots that grow in the crook between the main stem and a branch (called suckers) on indeterminate varieties. This directs energy toward fruit rather than extra foliage, though it's optional on determinate types.

## Common problems and what they mean

- **Yellowing lower leaves**: Often just normal aging, but check watering consistency if it spreads upward.
- **Blossom end rot** (a dark, sunken patch on the fruit's bottom): Usually a calcium uptake issue caused by inconsistent watering, not a soil calcium deficiency.
- **Cracked fruit**: A heavy rain or watering session after a dry spell — mulch helps even out soil moisture.
- **Leaf curl**: Often heat stress, though it's worth checking for aphids if curling comes with sticky residue.

With steady watering, a bit of support, and a sunny spot getting six or more hours of direct light, most tomato problems solve themselves by midsummer. The rest is mostly patience.

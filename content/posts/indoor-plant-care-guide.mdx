If you've killed a few houseplants and assumed you just don't have a green thumb, the more likely explanation is simpler: most plant care advice is generic, and your home's light and humidity are not. Here's what actually determines whether a houseplant thrives.

## The real culprit is usually overwatering

It feels intuitive to water a struggling plant more. In practice, soggy soil suffocates roots and invites rot, and a plant with damaged roots can't take up water even when it's sitting in plenty of it — so it wilts, which looks like thirst, which leads to more watering. Before adding water, stick a finger an inch or two into the soil. If it's still damp, wait.

## Match the plant to your light, not the other way around

"Bright indirect light" is the most common houseplant requirement, and the most misunderstood. It means a spot near a window but out of direct sun — not a windowless hallway. If your home genuinely has low light, choose plants suited to it (pothos, ZZ plants, snake plants) rather than fighting to keep a light-hungry fiddle leaf fig alive in a dim corner.

## Pots need drainage

A pot without a drainage hole makes consistent watering nearly impossible to get right, because excess water has nowhere to go. If you love a particular pot without drainage, use it as a decorative outer cover and keep the plant in a plastic nursery pot with holes inside it.

## Humidity matters more than people expect

Many popular houseplants — ferns, calatheas, most tropical foliage — originate in humid environments and struggle in the dry air of a heated or air-conditioned home. Grouping plants together, using a pebble tray, or running a small humidifier nearby can make a noticeable difference for humidity-loving species.

## A simple monthly rhythm

1. Check soil moisture for each plant before watering on your usual schedule — adjust rather than watering on autopilot.
2. Rotate pots a quarter turn so growth doesn't lean permanently toward the light.
3. Wipe dust off large leaves so plants can photosynthesize efficiently.
4. Look under leaves for early signs of pests like spider mites or mealybugs, which are far easier to treat early.

None of this requires a special talent. It requires paying attention to the specific plant in front of you, in the specific spot you've put it — which is really all a "green thumb" ever was.
